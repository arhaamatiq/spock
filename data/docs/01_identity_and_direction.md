## The shortest introduction

Arhaam Atiq is a software engineer and AI researcher studying Applied Mathematics & Computational Science at San José State University (expected graduation: December 2027). He builds reliable AI systems: infrastructure that executes experiments under failure, safeguards that constrain autonomous agents before they take consequential actions, and research tools that investigate what model evaluations actually measure. **His central thesis:** powerful intelligence matters only if the systems around it can be trusted. That means asking three questions that are often left until too late: *Can the action be controlled? Can the execution be reproduced? Can the reported result be believed?*

Those questions connect work that might otherwise look unrelated:

- **AgentGate** operates at the moment an AI agent turns a decision into an action. It checks real tool calls against authorized scope before allowing them to execute.
- **BreakWater** operates at the moment an AI evaluation turns execution into a verdict. It tests both the distributed machinery running experiments and the graders producing PASS/FAIL decisions.
- **LLM unlearning research** operates at the moment a behavioral metric is interpreted as scientific evidence. It separates a model's willingness to answer from its recovery of factual discrimination.
- **Applied Engineering** takes the same accountability into client delivery: not simply proposing an idea, but scoping it, managing a team, shipping it, and evaluating whether it works.

That combination—systems engineering, empirical ML, and the ability to organize people around a real outcome—is the most useful way to understand him.

## What makes his work distinctive

### He questions the measurement, not just the model

In AI, improvement is often expressed as a percentage on a leaderboard. Arhaam's work asks what produced that percentage. BreakWater independently recovered known weaknesses in HumanEval tests and investigated live Terminal-Bench verifier behavior. In unlearning research, he found that a model can become better at producing an answer without becoming proportionally better at distinguishing it from a plausible false answer. The common skill is not skepticism for its own sake; it is designing tests that make a claim falsifiable.

### He understands that reliability is an implementation problem

It is easy to diagram a robust system. It is harder to stop workers mid-execution and prove that an experiment still returns exactly one accepted result. BreakWater's local fault-injection run completed 96 of 96 logical trials despite simulated crashes and stalls, with six interrupted trials recovered and zero lost or duplicate result rows. The distinction is tangible: the claim was checked against database invariants, not inferred from a successful-looking demo.

### He works across boundaries

His projects span Python backend engineering, Docker and PostgreSQL coordination, AI-agent integrations, open-weight model evaluation on GPUs, research methodology, web-facing experiences, and team leadership. Breadth matters here because many AI failures occur *between* categories: between a model and its tools, a worker and a database, a test and its grader, or a prototype and the people expected to use it.

### He brings others into the work

Applied Engineering was founded around a problem he could see clearly: capable students need opportunities to produce real evidence of their ability, while organizations have software problems they could solve with well-scoped engineering teams. Building a 65+ member organization demanded more than writing code. It required recruiting, onboarding, aligning people, handling stakeholder expectations, and getting a client-facing product over the finish line.

### He treats communication as part of engineering

A system is not fully useful if its behavior cannot be explained, examined, or trusted by someone else. His projects repeatedly turn opaque outcomes into legible evidence: AgentGate's allow/block decisions and audit history; BreakWater's reproducible finding receipts; HireSignal's decomposed scoring; unlearning research's careful separation of what a metric does and does not establish.
